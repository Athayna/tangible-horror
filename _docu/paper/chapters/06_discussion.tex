\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Taken at face value, the results endorse passive haptics without reservation: each participant who played both versions reported more presence with the proxies, on all four subscales, and the differences dwarf what presence manipulations tend to produce. The direction matches the classic findings~\cite{Hoffman.1998, Insko.2001} and extends them to aversive materials; confirming a dreaded touch strengthened the illusion instead of breaking it. The item-level pattern adds internal plausibility, since the gains concentrate where a haptic proxy acts (feeling present in the space, consistency with real-world experience, reduced awareness of the real surroundings) and stay smallest where no proxy could help, on the item asking whether the virtual cabin seemed \emph{more} real than reality.

The size of the effect calls for caution. We could not blind participants to condition: one setup has props standing in the room and an experimenter holding a spray bottle. In a within-subject design this invites demand characteristics, and participants may have rated the contrast between conditions instead of each experience on its own. The low baseline scores point the same way. Subscale means of 2--3 are low for a functioning VR experience, which suggests participants used the questionnaire to compare, pushing the baseline down after (or in anticipation of) the richer haptic version. A between-subject replication, in which each participant sees one condition, would separate the haptic effect from this contrast effect. We would expect the difference to shrink there, though the concentration of gains on touch-related items gives some confidence it would survive.

The data also stays silent on one question. The IPQ measures presence, not fear or disgust, so whether the proxies made the experience scarier, our original motivation, remains unmeasured. Both
studies that manipulated aversive touch directly reached for physiological measures alongside self-report~\cite{Brice.2021, Cong.2025}; we have neither. The involvement gain of $+2.65$ hints at it, with the caveat noted in \ref{sec:discussion-limitations}. Participants with proxies reported less awareness of the room, the experimenters, and the surrounding sounds, and a horror experience depends on that narrowing of attention.
