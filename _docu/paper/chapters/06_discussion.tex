\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Taken at face value, the results endorse passive haptics without reservation: each participant who played both versions reported more presence with the proxies, on all four subscales. The direction matches the classic findings~\cite{Hoffman.1998, Insko.2001} and extends them to aversive materials; confirming a dreaded touch strengthened the illusion instead of breaking it. The item-level pattern adds internal plausibility, since the gains concentrate where a haptic proxy acts (feeling present in the space, consistency with real-world experience, reduced awareness of the real surroundings) and stay smallest where no proxy could help, on the item asking whether the virtual cabin seemed \emph{more} real than reality. This is also the pattern we predicted in Section~\ref{sec:study-measures}: the subscales a valid sensorimotor action should move gained most, and experienced realism, which a proxy for a rusted bowl of eyes cannot make plausible, gained least ($+2.05$ against $+3.12$ for spatial presence). General presence gained most of all ($+3.20$), which fits the same reading, since the item asks about the sense of being there.


The size of the effect calls for caution, and a comparison makes that concrete. Meehan et al.\ ran the same manipulation with 52 counterbalanced participants and a physical ledge; there the physiological and behavioural presence measures separated the conditions significantly, while the presence questionnaire reached only a trend ($p = .060$)~\cite{Meehan.2002}. Our five pairs separate on every subscale with $d_z$ above two. A questionnaire difference that large, from a manipulation whose classic study could barely move a questionnaire at all, is more plausibly a property of our design than of our proxies. We could not blind participants to condition: one setup has props standing in the room and an experimenter holding a spray bottle. The questionnaire itself made the manipulation salient: its first item asked participants to state which version they had just played, immediately before the presence items. Presence questionnaires are known to be read comparatively when participants have more than one VR experience to relate to~\cite{Schwind.2019, Graf.2020}. In a within-subject design this invites demand characteristics, and participants may have rated the contrast between conditions instead of each experience on its own. The low baseline scores point the same way. Subscale means of 2--3 are low for a functioning VR experience, which suggests participants used the questionnaire to compare, pushing the baseline down after (or in anticipation of) the richer haptic version. A between-subject replication, in which each participant sees one condition, would separate the haptic effect from this contrast effect. We would expect the difference to shrink there, though the concentration of gains on touch-related items gives some confidence it would survive.

The order effect points the same way. Meehan et al.\ report an orienting effect for the same manipulation, with every presence measure dropping after the first exposure~\cite{Meehan.2002}; our haptic-first participants rated both playthroughs more extremely than the baseline-first ones, which is what an orienting effect on the first exposure and a contrast effect on the second would jointly produce. The data also stays silent on one question. The IPQ measures presence, not fear or disgust, so whether the proxies made the experience scarier, our original motivation, remains unmeasured. Both studies that manipulated aversive touch directly reached for physiological measures alongside self-report~\cite{Brice.2021, Cong.2025}; we have neither. The involvement gain of $+2.65$ hints at it, with the caveat noted in~\ref{sec:discussion-limitations}. Participants with proxies reported less awareness of the room, the experimenters, and the surrounding sounds, and a horror experience depends on that narrowing of attention. The step from there to fear is nevertheless weaker than it looks. Presence and fear covary strongly in VR height exposure ($r = .62$), but the experimentally supported direction runs from fear to presence; manipulating presence did not by itself change fear responses~\cite{Gromer.2019}. In VR horror specifically, Lin reports plausibility as the stronger trigger of fear, and place illusion is the side a proxy acts on~\cite{Lin.2017}. What speaks for a fear effect is the ledge study, where a passive haptic raised the physiological anxiety response directly~\cite{Meehan.2002, Slater.2009}. A presence gain of the size we measured is therefore not a fear gain of any size, and our own data cannot close that gap.
